MAKING THE INTERVAL SCENARIO-AWARE: TWO APPROACHES, COMPARED HONESTLY

Once the model itself is better (slide 11), a sharper calibration
question remains: should the interval WIDTH itself vary by scenario, not
just be validated per scenario? Two approaches, built and compared:

Approach A — Mondrian (discrete group-conditional) conformal calibration:
  → Partition calibration data into the same real zone categories
    (turn / intersection / curved-road / open-road), fit a SEPARATE
    calibrated interval per group instead of one pooled number

(Plot: mondrian_vs_vanilla_width.png — grey dashed = pooled global width;
colored = per-group width)

Every group now lands close to the 90% target — the hidden gap from
slide 11 (before the retrain) is resolved. But: NOT a free win.
Size-weighted across how common each group actually is, steering and
traffic-light-confidence intervals are actually ~11-19% WIDER on average
under this scheme, not tighter — smaller per-group sample sizes need more
statistical padding. Real, honest cost, not hidden.

Approach B — continuous conditioning on the model's own learned scene
representation (no hand-defined groups):
  → Find the k most similar past scenes by the model's own internal
    representation of "what kind of situation is this," calibrate
    against just that neighborhood

(Plot: embedding_turn_scalar_bands.png, embedding_steering_width_vs_turn.png)

Distinguishing property discrete grouping structurally cannot match: this
approach improves AUTOMATICALLY when the underlying model improves — no
manual re-definition of group boundaries needed. Directly confirmed:
after the slide 11 retrain, BOTH the baseline and this approach's result
improved together with zero changes to the calibration scheme itself. It
can also tighten a feature where the pooled calibration was already fine,
not just widen where needed — Mondrian's fixed categories can't do that.
Honest cost: noisier per-step estimates (visible as a real, sudden
widening on an otherwise calm example) from having less data per estimate.

Where this leaves things: neither is a strictly better default — the
right choice likely depends on how much data is available and how
consequential the target scenario is. Both are documented, reproducible,
and available.
