This is the payload slide for Paper 1's fault-validation claim — the
independent literature review specifically flagged the multi-feature
diagnosticity finding as likely genuinely new (no located prior work
checks a calibrated signal against live faults AND asks which state
feature carries the clearest evidence). Say this confidently but
precisely: "likely new" based on a real, deliberate search, not "we
assume this is new."

The IMU-vs-TL distinction matters directly for how Layer 2 gets scoped
(slide 15) — a 3-second consequence-estimation horizon is well-matched to
TL's fast timescale and structurally cannot see IMU's 100+-second
compounding effect coming in time. This is presented here as a finding,
not yet as the Layer 2 design decision — that's slide 15's job.

The severity-sweep pilot result needs the confound explained honestly if
asked why it isn't cleaner: the existing fault variant used for this
first look ramps severity continuously WITHIN a single approach to an
intersection, so how severe the fault is and how close the vehicle is to
the actual decision point are entangled in that specific data. This
isn't a flaw in the finding, it's exactly the kind of thing careful
analysis is supposed to catch — and it directly justifies why the next
data collection (slide 15) uses a fixed-severity design instead.

If asked why all three calibration schemes are shown together here rather
than picking one: this is deliberately the same real fault data viewed
through each of slide 10-12's three methods at once, not a new dataset —
it's the connective tissue between the calibration-methodology section
and the fault-validation section, showing all three approaches are live,
comparable, and consistent with each other on data none of them were
tuned against. Mondrian's visible step-jumps line up with the same real
zone boundaries as slide 12; the embedding band's responsiveness here is
the same property demonstrated on nominal data in slide 12, just under a
harder test. Don't over-read the embedding band's tighter tracking as
proof it's "better" for fault detection specifically — that comparison
(which scheme actually detects faults best, at matched false-alarm rates)
hasn't been run yet; what this slide shows is that it's live and behaving
sensibly, not a head-to-head detection benchmark.
