Be precise about what's novel and what isn't if asked — this was checked
independently and deliberately, twice: once via a broad review of recent
Waymo safety-research publications (9 papers, read specifically for how
THEY structure a safety validation claim, not just for citations), and
once via a fresh agent explicitly tasked to find the closest prior work
and argue against novelty if it could. Neither individual technique here
is new — split-conformal prediction, group/Mondrian-conditional conformal
prediction, kNN/local conformal prediction, and AV fault-injection testing
are all independently established. What the review couldn't find anywhere
is this SPECIFIC combination, and specifically not the training-level fix
(most prior work treats a coverage gap as something to recalibrate around,
not retrain the model to close) or the live-fault validation with
cross-feature diagnosticity (which state feature carries the clearest
fault evidence, and it isn't always the obvious one — see slide 13).

Reuter et al. (ITSC 2026, arXiv:2605.19655) is worth naming explicitly and
by name if asked "isn't this already done" — it's the single closest paper
found, on a real AV, with the same core coverage-gap-hides-a-failure
finding. Citing it accurately and explaining the real differences is more
credible than not knowing it exists.

The Waymo "two traditions" framing (UQ-as-alarm vs. risk-aware-planning-
assumes-correct) is not this project's own invention — it's a real,
useful way the literature itself is structured, worth crediting as
observed-not-invented if asked. The novel claim is specifically the
BRIDGE: propagating a distribution-free, per-feature, geometry-grounded
calibrated signal through a consequence model, not the bare fact that
someone propagates uncertainty forward (some risk-aware planning work
already does that in a weaker sense — don't overclaim "nobody does this
at all").
